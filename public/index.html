An experiment with the Harvard Art Museums collection

Infinite Monkey Museum

A monkey paints random 16 × 16 pictures, very fast, using the ten colors that best describe a thousand paintings. Each attempt is compared with every painting in the collection. How close can chance get to art, and how much faster does it get there when good guesses are kept?

The statistics

How close does chance get?

Every grid the monkey paints is scored against every painting. Theory predicts what those scores should look like; here is how the monkey compares.

Ranking by surprise. A flat, mid-toned painting scores well against any noise, so raw similarity keeps picking the same few. Surprise asks instead how unusual a score is for that painting: z = (similarity − its average against noise) ÷ its spread, in standard deviations (σ). Under the theory every painting's z is a standard bell curve, so every painting has the same chance to be the match.

    Monkeys, museums and chance

    The infinite monkey theorem says that a monkey hitting keys at random for long enough will almost surely type any given text. Émile Borel used the image in 1913; in 1928 Arthur Eddington put it in a museum: If an army of monkeys were strumming on typewriters they might write all the books in the British Museum. This site asks the same of paintings, at the scale of a thumbnail. Even a 16 × 16 picture in ten colors has 10256 possibilities, far more than there are atoms in the universe. Chance alone never gets there, which the statistics above show live.

    Artists have long used chance on purpose. Jean Arp dropped torn paper squares and glued them where they fell (Collage with Squares Arranged according to the Laws of Chance, 1916–17). Ellsworth Kelly let chance procedures place the colored squares of Spectrum Colors Arranged by Chance (1951–53). Gerhard Richter's color charts, such as 256 Farben (1974), set out 256 colors in a grid in random order: the same number of cells as one of this monkey's grids. Those works are chance framed as art. The monkey runs the opposite test: can chance land on art that already exists?

    Richard Dawkins's weasel program (The Blind Watchmaker, 1986) showed why cumulative selection beats single-step chance. Random letters almost never spell METHINKS IT IS LIKE A WEASEL, but keeping the best of each generation of copies gets there in a few dozen steps. The Mutation mode does the same with a painting: it reaches an exact copy after tens of thousands of grids, where pure chance would need about 10256. The weasel knows its target, which is the point and the cheat. Evolution has no target; the comparison is about how fast keeping partial successes compounds.

    The best match after n tries grows slowly, roughly like √(2 ln n) standard deviations above average. That is the extreme value statistics of Fisher, Tippett and Gumbel. Each tenfold increase in monkeys buys less than the one before, and because one grid is scored against all thousand paintings, those thousand comparisons behave like only a few hundred independent tries.

    Finally, the experiment has a point of view built in. A measure of pixel similarity treats the empty silk of a Chinese or Japanese ink painting as missing information. In that tradition, empty space is part of the composition. Making the grids legible meant dropping most East Asian paintings, and the ten-color palette is the brown of oil paint, old varnish and aged paper. The Findings above show what that leaves.
